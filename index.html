Current Conditions — Pawleys Island

Updated weekly · Pier, Surf & Inshore · Spring 2026

Red drum and flounder are showing up in the creek mouths and around the pier pilings as water temps climb toward 65°F. Spanish mackerel runs have been sporadic but consistent on incoming tides around the pier end. Sheepshead are stacked tight on the pier structure — use live fiddler crabs for best results.

The surf has been productive for pompano in the mornings. Spot and whiting are plentiful along the beach. Bluefish blitzes in the evenings have been drawing crowds to the pier.

🐟 Tip: Fish the first two hours of an incoming tide for the best action — especially around the pier structure and creek junctions.

Fall is arguably the finest fishing on the SC coast. Giant redfish bull reds stage in the inlets through October. Flounder flood the flats before the run-out. Don't miss it.

Know Your Species

Red Drum (Redfish)
Sciaenops ocellatus
Best season: Fall (bull reds) & Spring. Inshore all year. Fish creek mouths, oyster beds, and around the pier on incoming tides. The black spot near the tail base is its signature mark.

Limit (SC): 2 per person/day · max 6 per boat · 15–23" slot (release fish outside slot). Reduced from 3/day in 2018.
Live CrabFinger MulletGold Spoon
Southern Flounder
Paralichthys lethostigma
Best season: Fall is prime — flounder migrate toward inlets in Oct–Nov. Drift live finger mullet or mud minnows along grass flat edges and near inlet mouths. Both eyes on the same side is the unmistakable tell.

Limit (SC): 5 per person/day · max 10 per boat · 16" minimum total length (updated 2021).
Finger MulletMud MinnowFlounder Rig
Spanish Mackerel
Scomberomorus maculatus
Best season: Spring through Fall. Mackerel arrive with warming water in March and run hard through October. Troll spoons or cast Clark spoons off the pier end on incoming tide. The gold oval spots on silver sides are the key ID mark.

Limit (SC): 15 per person/day · 12" fork length minimum.
Clark SpoonGotcha PlugLive Bait
Sheepshead
Archosargus probatocephalus
Best season: Winter through Spring. Sheepshead stack tight on the pier pilings and bridge structure year-round. Use live fiddler crabs on a small hook right against the structure. The bold black stripes and human-like front teeth are unmistakable.

Limit (SC): 10 per person/day · max 30 per boat · 12" minimum total length.
Fiddler CrabSand FleaOyster
Florida Pompano
Trachinotus carolinus
Best season: Spring (April–May) and Fall. Pompano run the surf hard after storms and on incoming tides. Walk the wash zone with a pompano rig and sand fleas (mole crabs). Blunt rounded head and deeply forked tail distinguish it from permit.

Limit (SC): 20 per person/day · no confirmed minimum size.

Speckled Trout
Cynoscion nebulosus
Best season: Late Fall through Winter in the creeks; early morning Spring near the pier. Work a suspending twitch bait along a grass flat edge. The two prominent canine fangs and black spots on the back and dorsal fin are its defining features.

Limit (SC): 10 per person/day · 14" minimum total length.

Est. 1711 · Georgetown County, SC

Three Centuries of
Sand, Salt & Story

Pawleys Island isn't just a place on a map. It's one of the oldest seaside resorts in America — a four-mile barrier island where planters escaped the fever, pirates lurked offshore, and ghosts still walk the beach before a storm.

c. 1711 — The Beginning
Percival Pawley Claims the Island
English colonist Percival Pawley received a land grant along the South Carolina coast and lent his name to the slender barrier island flanked by creek and sea. Georgetown County was already producing rice that fed the Atlantic world, and the Pawleys family used the island as a seasonal retreat — one of the earliest in the New World.
1700s–1800s — The Planter Era
Rice, Wealth & the Summer Escape
Georgetown County's tidal rice fields made its planters some of the wealthiest people in colonial America — and also some of the most vulnerable to malaria. Every summer, families packed up and fled to Pawleys Island, where ocean breezes kept the mosquitoes at bay. The island became a close-knit colony of planter families, a world of open porches, hammocks, and salt air — a tradition that never really ended.
1898 — The Hammock is Born
The Original Rope Hammock, Made Right Here
Pawleys Island riverboat captain Joshua John Ward grew frustrated with the stiff, uncomfortable hammocks of his era and hand-wove a loosely slung rope hammock that conformed to the body. The design was so perfect it's barely changed in 125 years. The Original Pawleys Island Rope Hammock, still made locally at The Hammock Shop, became one of the most iconic products ever to come out of South Carolina — and a symbol of the island's unhurried philosophy.
1954 & 1989 — Storms of a Lifetime
Hurricane Hazel & Hugo: The Island Endures
Hurricane Hazel made landfall near Pawleys Island on October 15, 1954 — one of the most powerful Atlantic hurricanes ever recorded at Category 4. Entire oceanfront rows were swept clean. Then, 35 years later, Hurricane Hugo (1989) struck with even greater fury, a Category 4 with 135 mph winds. Each time, Pawleys Island rebuilt, quietly and without drama. The island's resilience is part of its character — battered but unbowed, arrogantly shabby to the end.
Today — "Arrogantly Shabby"
A Place Stubbornly Resistant to Change
Pawleys Island has long embraced the unofficial motto "Arrogantly Shabby" — a phrase that captures its refusal to be glamorized. No neon, no chain restaurants, no high-rises. Just creaking screen doors, weathered cottages on stilts, the smell of low tide, and the same families who have been coming for generations. In a world obsessed with newness, Pawleys Island remains defiantly, beautifully old.
Local Legends & Lore

The Island Keeps Its Secrets

Three centuries leave marks that don't wash away with the tide. Some stories are history. Some are something else entirely.

Ghost Story
The Gray Man of Pawleys Island
South Carolina's Most Famous Ghost

He appears at dusk, walking the beach in tattered gray clothes, just before a hurricane makes landfall. Residents who encounter him — and heed his wordless warning to leave — return to find their homes untouched amid the destruction all around. Those who ignore him are not so fortunate.

The legend traces back to the early 1800s, when a young man drowned in the marshes rushing to reunite with his fiancée after a long journey. He has since appeared before Hurricane Hazel (1954), Hugo (1989), and several storms since — always on the same stretch of beach, always at twilight, always alone.

"He was wearing gray — gray pants, gray shirt — and he just looked at me. Then he walked toward the dunes and disappeared. The next morning, we left. Our neighbors didn't. Their house was gone."
— Local resident, 1989
Lost Love
Alice of the Hermitage
The Girl Who Died for a Ring

Alice Flagg was sixteen, beautiful, and desperately in love with a merchant her family considered far beneath her station. She wore his engagement ring on a ribbon beneath her dress — hidden from her powerful brother, Dr. Allard Flagg, who governed the plantation called The Hermitage on Murrells Inlet, just north of Pawleys.

When Alice fell gravely ill with typhoid fever in 1849, her brother stripped the ring from her finger before her family could see it. She died days later, whispering for it. She was buried at All Saints Episcopal Church — and to this day, visitors walk three times backward around her grave to summon her, often leaving rings at the headstone.

"The ring was never found. But on quiet nights near the old cemetery, a girl in white has been seen drifting between the graves — her hands searching, always searching."
Pirate History
Blackbeard's Coast
When the Waccamaw Neck Was Pirate Country

In the early 1700s, the shallow inlets and dense marshes of the South Carolina coast were a pirate's paradise. Edward Teach — Blackbeard — and "Gentleman Pirate" Stede Bonnet prowled these waters, raiding merchant vessels and disappearing into creeks too shallow for pursuing naval frigates.

Bonnet was famously captured in the Cape Fear River in 1718 after a pitched battle, tried in Charleston, and hanged. But the legend of buried treasure along the Pawleys shoreline — gold doubloons and Spanish silver hastily buried before a chase — has sent generations of locals probing the dunes with shovels after a good storm rearranges the sand.

"Nobody has ever found Blackbeard's treasure on Pawleys Island. But nobody has proven it isn't there, either."
Island Identity
"Arrogantly Shabby"
A Motto Born from Pure Stubbornness

Sometime in the mid-20th century, as neighboring Grand Strand communities raced to build hotels, arcades, and souvenir shops, someone put a sign on Pawleys Island that said it all: Arrogantly Shabby. It wasn't an apology. It was a declaration.

The island had no streetlights, no boardwalk, no resort development — and wanted none. Families who had been coming for a hundred years saw no reason to change anything. The cottages sagged pleasantly on their stilts. The docks weathered silver. The hammocks swayed. And that, the islanders felt, was precisely the point.

"On Pawleys Island, the whole idea is to do as little as possible, as slowly as possible, for as long as possible — and to feel absolutely no guilt about it whatsoever."
